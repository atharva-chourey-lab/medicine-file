# The canon travelled west: dating the Ayurvedic saṃhitās against the Baghdad, Chinese and Tibetan records

Author: Atharva Chourey
Affiliation: Atharva Chourey Lab (independent research lab)

*Manuscript in preparation — not yet peer-reviewed.*

## Abstract

The great Ayurvedic saṃhitās were translated in Baghdad and quoted there by 850 CE, yet no Chinese bibliography records a translation of Caraka or Suśruta, and the Tibetan canon took only Vāgbhaṭa's epitome. This paper dates the Ayurvedic canon — Caraka, Suśruta, Vāgbhaṭa, Bhela and Kāśyapa, with the Bower Manuscript as a physical anchor — against foreign dated records: Chinese catalogues, pilgrims and annals; Arabic bibliographies and compendia; Tibetan translation records; and dated manuscripts. A foreign record dates the event it directly records — a translation, a catalogue entry, a sojourn — and constrains the Indian original only as a bound, never as a composition date. On that rule, Caraka is BOUNDED, its layers running c. 100 BCE–200 CE and its present form complete by c. 500, with the *Firdaws al-Ḥikma* (c. 850) as its hard foreign terminus; Suśruta is BOUNDED, in present form by c. 500, translated by Mankha for Yaḥyā al-Barmakī in 786–803 and surviving complete in a manuscript dated 878; and Vāgbhaṭa is CORROBORATED, his window of c. 550–650 tightened from above by Yijing, who in 691 reports the eight branches lately compiled into one fascicle. What reached China was Ayurveda as practice and formulary — the Sui bibliography of 656 lists eleven Indian medical titles and pointedly not Caraka or Suśruta, Faxian describes physician-staffed dispensaries at Pāṭaliputra in 405–411, the *Waitai Miyao* of 752 carries an Indian eye treatise and cataract couching, and Liu Yuxi addressed a poem to his Brahmin eye-doctor in 837. The claim that Ayurveda is 5,000 years old is graded NO BITE as stated: decomposed, medical practice in India is ancient and undatable as a system, the Atharvavedic healing stratum dates to c. 1000 BCE, and the systematised canon to c. 200 BCE.

*Keywords:* Ayurveda; Caraka Saṃhitā; Suśruta Saṃhitā; Vāgbhaṭa; Bower Manuscript; history of medicine; Chinese records; Arabic translation; Tibetan canon

## 1. Introduction and method

Traditional dates for the Ayurvedic canon are tested here against foreign dated records: Chinese catalogues, pilgrims and annals; Arabic bibliographies and compendia; Tibetan translation colophons; and dated physical manuscripts. The verdict classes are CORRECTED, CORROBORATED, BOUNDED and NO BITE.

The discipline of the method is stated at the outset. A foreign record dates the event it directly records — a translation, a catalogue entry, a sojourn — and constrains the Indian original only as a bound, never as a composition date. A translation made in Baghdad in 786–803 proves that its Sanskrit original existed, complete enough to translate whole, by 803 at the latest; it does not prove when that original was composed. A catalogue that lists a title proves the title was present to the cataloguers; a catalogue that does not list a title, where listing is the catalogue's business, is evidence about transmission, handled as such and no further.

The dating evidence, it turns out, follows a geographic split, and that split is the paper's structural finding.

## 2. The structural finding: the canon travelled west, not east

The great saṃhitās travelled west, not east. Caraka and Suśruta were translated into Arabic in Baghdad and quoted there by 850 CE; no Chinese bibliography ever records a translation of either, and the Tibetan canon took only Vāgbhaṭa's epitome.

What reached China was Ayurveda as *practice and formulary* — Jīvaka-attributed prescriptions, an Indian eye treatise, a massage method, court physicians — carried inside Buddhist and commercial traffic, not the books themselves.

The dating evidence follows the same split. The canon's hard foreign anchors are Arabic. China's anchors date the *system's presence and prestige*, and one of them — Yijing, writing in 691 — turns out to be the sharpest bracket on Vāgbhaṭa that exists anywhere.

## 3. Caraka Saṃhitā

### 3.1 The tradition and its layers

Caraka, redactor of Agniveśa's tantra, was by tradition court physician to Kaniṣka. The text's own colophons give the layering plainly: Agniveśa composed; Caraka redacted; Dṛḍhabala of Pañcanadapura restored the lost final third — the last seventeen chapters of the Cikitsāsthāna and the whole Kalpa- and Siddhisthāna — centuries later.

An earlier bound on the Kaniṣka epoch confines it to c. 125–165 CE, excluding the old 78 CE equation. Taken at face value, the tradition therefore no longer puts Caraka in the 1st century: it puts the *redaction layer* in the mid-2nd century CE — which is where the text's independent internal window already sits. The bound corroborates the scholarly window; it does not prove the identification, for three reasons:

- The tradition's only early carrier is itself foreign and late: the Chinese *Sūtrālaṃkāra* (T.201), translated by Kumārajīva's bureau c. 402–413, where a physician named Caraka attends Kaniṣka alongside Māṭhara and Aśvaghoṣa. The story is attested some 250 years after the event it claims.
- "Caraka" may name a school or a professional type — the wandering physician-teacher — rather than one man; the Indian tradition itself also made him an incarnation of Śeṣa and identified him with Patañjali. The name attracts identifications.
- Even if the identification holds, it dates one *layer*. The Agniveśa core behind it is older by an amount no record measures.

The Kaniṣka's-physician tradition is therefore unprovable on the present record.

### 3.2 Foreign termini

The Arabic route is the hard one. ʿAlī ibn Rabban al-Ṭabarī's *Firdaws al-Ḥikma* (c. 850) devotes the closing section of its seventh part to a 36-chapter summary of Indian medicine drawn, per Meyerhof's analysis, from four named books: Charaka (Jarak), Suśruta (Susrud), the Nidāna, and the Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya. A full Arabic Charaka existed to be summarised by 850. Ibn al-Nadīm's *Fihrist* (987) lists Sharaka among the Indian medical books translated in the Barmakid-era movement; the Charaka translation is credited to ʿAbdallāh ibn ʿAlī, 9th century, via Persian. Al-Rāzī's *Ḥāwī* (c. 900) quotes Sharaka as a routine authority.

The Chinese trace is thin, and honestly so. No Chinese translation of the Caraka Saṃhitā is recorded in the Sui catalogue or any Tang bibliography. The name reaches China only as Kaniṣka's courtier in the story collection above. One indirect content trace exists: Sun Simiao's *Qianjin Yaofang* (652) quotes "the great Indian physician Jīvaka" — *there is nothing in the world that cannot be used as medicine* — a line traced to Caraka Saṃhitā, Sūtrasthāna 26.12, arriving under Jīvaka's name, not Caraka's.

No complete Tibetan translation of the Caraka Saṃhitā exists in the Tengyur. The canon that Tibet actually absorbed was Vāgbhaṭa (§5).

### 3.3 Window and verdict

**Tightest defensible window.** Core layers c. 100 BCE–200 CE; the Caraka redaction, *if* the Kaniṣka identification is granted, c. 125–165; Dṛḍhabala's completion 300–500; **present form by c. 500**, with the formulary tradition already excerpting Caraka-line material in the Bower Manuscript (c. 450–550, §7). Hard foreign terminus ante quem: **850** (Arabic).

**Verdict: BOUNDED** for the Kaniṣka tradition; **CORROBORATED** for the text's existence and present form well before the Arabic terminus. Strongest foreign anchor: the *Firdaws al-Ḥikma*, c. 850.

## 4. Suśruta Saṃhitā

### 4.1 The tradition and the layered text

Suśruta, son of Viśvāmitra, learned medicine by tradition from Divodāsa, king of Kāśi — a frame that would put the text in the Vedic age. Nothing foreign, epigraphic or physical reaches a historical Suśruta; the frame is a teaching lineage, not a date. **Verdict on the Vedic-antiquity claim: NO BITE.**

The layered text, on Meulenbeld's assessment, the scholarly baseline used here, is as follows: composition began in the last centuries BCE; an old five-book version was redacted and the long sixth book, the Uttaratantra, added by a later hand; the work was known to Dṛḍhabala (fl. 300–500), giving the **present form by c. 500 CE**. The oldest surviving manuscript — Kathmandu KL 699, palm-leaf, dated **878 CE** — already contains the complete six-book text, Uttaratantra included: a physical terminus for the finished form. It is the oldest dated manuscript of the work.

### 4.2 The Nāgārjuna-as-redactor tradition, tested

Manuscript colophons and medieval commentators name a redactor Nāgārjuna; the 1952 Chronology Committee of the National Institute of Sciences of India placed "the recension of the Suśruta Saṃhitā by Nāgārjuna" in the 3rd–4th centuries CE. An earlier bound puts the Mādhyamika Nāgārjuna's floruit at c. 150–250, with a hard terminus of 409. Three grades follow:

- *If* the redactor were the Mādhyamika, the redaction would date to c. 150–250 — inside the text's growth period, so not absurd on chronology alone.
- But the identification itself is the weak link: Meulenbeld's assessment is that the 2nd-century-Nāgārjuna redaction has been disproved; the name Nāgārjuna attaches to several later figures, the alchemical Nāgārjuna of the rasaśāstra tradition among them, and the committee's own 3rd–4th-century placement already concedes it is not obviously the Mādhyamika.
- Even a genuine 2nd- or 3rd-century redaction would not date the present text, whose Uttaratantra layer runs later, complete by c. 500.

**Verdict: the identification — NO BITE** (unprovable, and on current philology rejected); **the text — BOUNDED**.

### 4.3 Foreign terminus

The Arabic *Kitab-i-Susrud* is the canon's cleanest translation anchor: the *Fihrist* records that **Mankha (Manka), an Indian physician at the ʿAbbāsid court, translated Suśruta from Sanskrit for Yaḥyā ibn Khālid al-Barmakī**. Yaḥyā fell in 803, so the translation falls in **786–803**. Mankha is independently placed at Hārūn al-Rashīd's court; he is remembered for curing the caliph. By 850 the *Firdaws* is summarising Susrud alongside Charaka.

**Tightest defensible window.** Core from the last centuries BCE; **present form by c. 500**; oldest dated manuscript 878, complete. **Verdict: BOUNDED.** Strongest foreign anchor: Mankha's translation, 786–803 — Suśruta existed, complete enough to translate whole, by 803 at the latest, some three centuries after the internal evidence already closes its present form. Like all translation termini, it confirms; it does not tighten.

## 5. Vāgbhaṭa — Aṣṭāṅgasaṅgraha and Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya

The scholarly window is c. 600–650 in its narrow form — Meulenbeld: c. 600; estimates run 550–650; whether one Vāgbhaṭa wrote both works is itself disputed. The foreign records bracket it from three sides, and one of them is the single best dating instrument in this paper.

- **Yijing, writing in 691** (*Nanhai Jigui Neifa Zhuan*, vol. 3, §27, "On understanding the origin of disease"): the eight branches of Indian medicine "formerly existed in eight books, but lately a man has compiled them into one fascicle" — and Yijing describes the resulting single-volume epitome as the current state of the art. The standard scholarly reading, from Takakusu's translation onward, identifies the unnamed compiler's epitome with Vāgbhaṭa's Aṣṭāṅga synthesis. The Chinese does not name him, so the identification is graded probable, not firm — he names no one — but a dated eyewitness in India in 673–685 reporting the eight-branch epitome as *recent* is precisely a floruit bracket: the synthesis existed by 691 and was new enough to remark on. It fits c. 600–650 and presses against anything much later.
- **Mādhava's Nidāna (c. 700)** borrows verses from the Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya wholesale — Indian-internal evidence — and the *Firdaws* (850) already summarises the Hṛdaya in Arabic. The "Ashtankar" of the *Fihrist* (987) is most plausibly this same Aṣṭāṅga work, or its twin the Saṅgraha, in Arabic dress. The identification of "Ashtankar" specifically has been debated; after the *Firdaws* attestation it is corroborative, not load-bearing.
- **Tibetan translation**: the Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya is the one great medical saṃhitā the Tibetan canon took — Tengyur, Toh 4310, with Candranandana's commentary — translated in the later-diffusion enterprise associated with Rinchen Zangpo (958–1055). A terminus of c. 1055: real, but the loosest of the three brackets.
- From below: Vāgbhaṭa epitomises Caraka and Suśruta in their Dṛḍhabala-era forms, so he stands after c. 500.

**Tightest defensible window: c. 550–650**, with Yijing (691, "lately compiled into one fascicle") as the forcing foreign record at the top and the post-500 state of his sources at the bottom. **Verdict: CORROBORATED** — the one canon author whose traditional scholarly window the foreign record actively tightens rather than merely permits.

## 6. The minor saṃhitās

### 6.1 Bhela Saṃhitā

Bhela, fellow-pupil of Agniveśa under Ātreya, survives in a single fragmentary Tanjore manuscript. No Arabic, Chinese or Tibetan attestation of the book exists. What dates the *tradition* is physical: the Bower Manuscript's medical parts excerpt Bhela-line material (§7), so a Bhela formulary tradition existed by c. 450–550. The present text is layered on older material and is commonly placed in the 6th–7th centuries in surviving form.

**Verdict: BOUNDED** — tradition by c. 500; present text later and only loosely placed. Strongest anchor: the Bower Manuscript.

### 6.2 Kāśyapa Saṃhitā (Vṛddhajīvakīya Tantra)

The paediatrics tantra taught by Mārīci Kāśyapa, composed by Vṛddhajīvaka and redacted by Vātsya — surviving, like Bhela, in a single incomplete manuscript (Kathmandu). Traditional datings — 6th century BCE for the tantra — have no foreign or physical support; scholarly placements put a core in the early centuries CE, Vātsya's redaction later, often c. 7th century, with possible retouching later still. No foreign record names the text.

**Verdict: NO BITE** — the web does not reach it; its dates remain an internal-Indian argument.

## 7. The Bower Manuscript — the physical anchor

Found in a ruined stūpa near Kucha in 1890: seven birch-bark treatises in Gupta script, three of them medical — a medical fragment (Part I), the **Navanītaka** formulary (Part II), and a second medical fragment (Part III). It is dated palaeographically to **c. 450–550** — Sander: 500–550; Callewaert: c. 450; Hoernle's final view 4th–5th century; no radiocarbon date has been published. What it proves by its date:

- A Sanskrit *formulary* tradition — planned in sixteen chapters, opening with a recipe that also stands in the Caraka Saṃhitā, and drawing on Caraka-, Suśruta- and Bhela-line material — was mature, portable, and being copied on the northern Silk Road by c. 500.
- It is a terminus not for any one saṃhitā's final form but for the *system behind them*: the eight-branch medicine the pilgrims describe was already a bookish, excerptable corpus in the Gupta age.

**Verdict: CORROBORATED** as an anchor — physical, dated by script; probable, not firm, because palaeographic.

## 8. The spine biting directly — Indian medicine inside dated Chinese records

The records in this section date the system's presence, practice and prestige in and for China. They date no saṃhitā directly, except where §5 has already used Yijing's 691 report as Vāgbhaṭa's bracket.

### 8.1 Faxian, Pāṭaliputra, c. 405–411

Faxian's *Foguo Ji* is the earliest dated eyewitness to organised Indian medicine: in the capital, the Vaiśya elders and householders have established houses for dispensing charity and medicine, where the poor and destitute, orphans, widowers and childless, the maimed and cripples, and the diseased come; the physicians examine their diseases; they get food and medicines as needed, and all at no cost. This is an *infrastructure* anchor: by c. 405–411, institutional, charitable, physician-staffed medical provision existed in a major Indian city — the social footprint of the system the saṃhitās describe, observed within Dṛḍhabala's own floruit window. It dates no text; it dates the world the texts served. **Firm.**

### 8.2 The Sui shu bibliography, compiled 641–656

The *Sui shu* bibliography lists, among the medical titles of the imperial collection, a shelf of Indian works in Chinese dress — the direct hit on Indian medical *literature* in China. The titles named in the source dossier are:

- *Pharmacopeia of the Brahmin Ṛṣis*, 20 juan
- *Brahmin Pharmacopeia*, 5 juan
- *Pharmacopeia of Nāgārjuna Bodhisattva*, 4 juan
- *Pharmacopeia Spoken by the Ṛṣis of the Western Regions*
- *Essential Formulas Collected by Famous Physicians of the Western Regions*, 4 juan
- *Formulas on Fate Expounded by Jīvaka*, 2 juan
- *Gāndhārī Formulas for Treating Demons*, 10 juan

— **eleven Indian medical titles in all by the usual count**, most inherited from the Liang imperial catalogue. What existence by 656 proves: a substantial Indian formulary literature had been translated or compiled in Chinese by the 6th–7th centuries — generic Brahmin and ṛṣi pharmacopeias, a Nāgārjuna-branded drug book (the alchemical/medical Nāgārjuna, note, not evidence about the Mādhyamika), and Jīvaka material. What it does not prove: the presence of Caraka or Suśruta in China. Neither is listed, here or anywhere — the bibliography lists eleven Indian medical titles and pointedly not Caraka or Suśruta. **Firm as a catalogue; the titles are lost, so it dates a transmission, not their contents.**

### 8.3 Xuanzang, 646

Xuanzang's *Da Tang Xiyu Ji* (juan 2) gives the standard Indian curriculum as the five sciences — grammar, crafts, **cikitsā-vidyā (the science of medicine)**, logic, and inner science. Medicine as a formal, teachable śāstra in India, attested by a dated eyewitness. **Firm.**

### 8.4 Sun Simiao, *Qianjin Yaofang*, 652

Indian material stands inside the greatest Chinese formulary of the age, under Indian names: formulas attributed to Jīvaka, including the Jīvaka myriad-disease pill; the Jīvaka saying traced to Caraka, Sūtrasthāna 26.12 (§3); transliterated drug lore — the Agada pill, Kāmalā for jaundice; and a whole regimen, the **"Brahmin method" of massage and the eighteen postures of Tianzhu**, filed as Indian health practice. Dated Chinese attestation that Indian formulary and body-practice material was circulating in China by **652**. **Firm.**

### 8.5 Su Jing's *Xinxiu Bencao*, 659

The imperial Tang pharmacopoeia records a **"Brahmin's prescription"** as the known cure for beriberi, in all probability carried by an Indian Buddhist monk. An Indian formula, official in China by 659. **Firm.**

### 8.6 Nārāyaṇasvāmin at the Tang court, 648–649

The annals record that Wang Xuance's returning mission brought the Magadhan brahmin Nārāyaṇasvāmin, who compounded a longevity drug for Taizong under imperial patronage — the emperor died in 649, and the annalists let the elixir hang in the air next to the death. Gaozong's reign adds the East Indian monk-physician Lokāditya, dispatched to gather rare drugs from the southern seas. Whatever the pharmacology, the *prestige* is the datum: in 648 the Tang throne imported an Indian physician for the most sensitive commission in medicine. **Probable** — annalistic, and the elixir story is told with a moral.

### 8.7 Yijing, 691

Beyond the Vāgbhaṭa bracket (§5), Yijing's *Record* (§§27–29 of vol. 3) is the fullest dated foreign description of Indian medicine as practised: the eight branches named; diagnosis by examination; fasting as first therapy; Indian drugs compared with Chinese ones item by item; the rule that a physician who has mastered the eight branches may practise and be honoured. He also practised medicine himself on the voyage communities. Indian medicine in 691, from a resident eyewitness: a complete, book-based, state-recognised profession. **Firm.**

### 8.8 Wang Tao, *Waitai Miyao*, 752

The Tang official formulary — 6,900 prescriptions, sources named — is saturated with the Indian material at one further remove: remedies attributed to Jīvaka throughout; and its eye section (juan 21) opens with the **Preface to the Indian Treatise on Eyes** by Master Xie of Longshang, who says he received the Indian eye-doctrine from a Western monk — followed by the first detailed Chinese account of the **golden-scalpel cataract operation**, the couching procedure of the Suśruta tradition. A large share of all Indian-derived prescriptions preserved in Chinese literature is concentrated in this one dated book. **Firm** — Indian ophthalmology existed as a *text* in China by 752.

### 8.9 Liu Yuxi and the Brahmin eye-doctor, c. 836–837

The poet Liu Yuxi (772–842), himself medically literate, suffered three years of failing sight and addressed a poem to his surgeon: "Presented to the Brahmin Monk Eye-Doctor" — "the master has the art of the golden scalpel; how shall it lift my blindness?" A companion poem of winter **837** celebrates the cure: "the golden scalpel is no longer needed". An Indian oculist, practising the signature operation of the Suśruta tradition on a named, datable Chinese patient, in the 830s. **Probable** — the poems are datable only through Liu's biography and the 837 companion piece.

## 9. The headline claim — "Ayurveda is 5,000 years old"

The claim dissolves, under the web, into three different claims with three different oldest defensible dates.

**(a) Medical practice in India.** Genuinely ancient and genuinely undatable as a system. Drilled molars at Neolithic Mehrgarh (c. 7000 BCE) show interventionist dentistry on the subcontinent nine thousand years ago; nobody can call it Ayurveda without smuggling the later system into a nameless practice. *Oldest defensible date: none — practice, not a system.*

**(b) The Atharvavedic healing stratum.** The Atharvaveda's healing hymns — against fever (takman), worms, poison, fracture, with plant lore and the figure of the healer (bhiṣaj) — belong to the Atharvaveda's composition horizon, conventionally c. 1200–800 BCE. That is a healing-*hymn* stratum: no tridoṣa theory, no eight branches, no saṃhitās. *Oldest defensible date for this layer: c. 1000 BCE, ± two centuries — roughly 3,000 years, not 5,000.*

**(c) The systematised canon.** The eight-branch, tridoṣa system of the saṃhitās: layers from the last centuries BCE, Caraka and Suśruta in present form by c. 500 CE, excerpted on the Silk Road by c. 500 (Bower), described complete by Yijing in 691, translated in Baghdad by 803–850. *Oldest defensible date for the system: c. 200 BCE — roughly 2,200 years; for the texts as we have them, about 1,500–1,800 years.*

**Grade: NO BITE as stated.** In its usual form — the *system*, the *canon*, is 5,000 years old — the claim overshoots the systematised canon by some 2,800 years and the Atharvavedic stratum by some 2,000. What survives correction is still substantial: a three-millennium documented healing tradition, two millennia of it systematic. The 5,000-year figure is not an error of detail; it is the conflation of (a), (b) and (c) into a single birthday none of them has.

## 11. What would change these grades

- *Caraka–Kaniṣka:* an Indian-side attestation of the court-physician story independent of the Buddhist story literature — an inscription, a Kuṣāṇa-era colophon — would raise it from BOUNDED to CORROBORATED; a dated pre-Kuṣāṇa manuscript of the Caraka redaction would kill the identification outright.
- *Suśruta–Nāgārjuna:* a manuscript of the five-book Suśruta with a colophon naming its redactor and a date, or any pre-500 quotation naming Nāgārjuna in that role, would reopen the identification.
- *Vāgbhaṭa:* a Chinese or Tibetan witness that *names* the epitomist Yijing leaves anonymous would make the 691 bracket firm.
- *Bower:* a published radiocarbon date for the leaves would convert the file's central physical anchor from probable to firm — and move every bound that leans on it.
- *The canon in China:* a Dunhuang or Turfan fragment of an actual Caraka or Suśruta translation would overturn §2's structural finding; the catalogues say none was ever registered.
